# Ground truth for the sample logs

These logs are **synthetic**, generated by `generate.py` with a fixed seed. They are
not captures from a real system. The generator is committed so that the data is
reproducible and its origin is stated rather than implied.

The reason to generate rather than capture: the ground truth is known. Testing a
detector against a real log is nearly impossible, because you do not know what is in
it. You end up asserting "found 7 things" without ever checking whether the 7 were
the right 7.

Every address in these files is either private (RFC 1918) or from a reserved
documentation range (RFC 5737: `192.0.2.0/24`, `198.51.100.0/24`, `203.0.113.0/24`).
None of them routes to a real machine. That is deliberate: these addresses are
labelled as attackers in a public repository, and inventing plausible-looking ones
means eventually accusing a real person or company of something they did not do.

Regenerate with:

```bash
python samples/generate.py
```

---

## `auth_clean.log` — 107 lines

Five days of ordinary activity. **Expected findings: none.**

This is the false positive test and the most important file here. It contains, on
purpose, the cases that break naive rules:

| Content | Breaks |
|---|---|
| `ayden` mistypes twice, then succeeds | A rule alerting on any failure before a success |
| 4 failures from `192.168.1.45`, hours apart over the week | A rule totalling failures per day instead of per 60s window |
| `deploy` logs in by key 12 times a day, all successful | A rule keyed on volume rather than failures |
| Single failed attempts from 5 unrelated addresses | A rule with no minimum count |
| `sudo`, `CRON`, `systemd-logind`, disconnect messages | A parser that cannot skip non-auth lines |

## `auth_bruteforce.log` — 58 lines

Attacker `203.0.113.9` alternates `root` and `admin`, 46 failures over about four
minutes at roughly 5-second intervals, then **succeeds as `root`**. A `sudo wget` of
a remote script follows, so the log tells a complete story.

| Rule | Expected |
|---|---|
| `brute_force` | 1 finding, `203.0.113.9`, severity **high**, 46 failures, peak 12 in 60s |
| `success_after_failures` | 1 finding, user `root`, 46 prior failures |
| `password_spray` | **0 findings** — only 2 usernames, far below the 10 a spray needs |

That last row is the one to check. A spray rule that counts distinct usernames
without the per-user cap fires here and mislabels every brute force as a spray.

## `auth_spray.log` — 38 lines

Attacker `198.51.100.7` tries two passwords against 14 accounts over about eight
minutes, 17 seconds apart.

| Rule | Expected |
|---|---|
| `password_spray` | 1 finding, 14 accounts, max 2 attempts each, severity **medium** |
| `brute_force` | **0 findings** — 28 failures, but never 5 inside any 60-second window |
| `success_after_failures` | 0 findings — nothing landed |

The `brute_force` row is the reason this attack exists in the real world. Spread the
attempts out and a brute-force-only detector sees nothing.

## `windows_security.csv` — 30 rows

Attacker `10.0.0.66` over RDP: 24 failures against `administrator` and `admin`, 7
seconds apart, then a **successful logon as `administrator`**.

| Rule | Expected |
|---|---|
| `brute_force` | 1 finding, `10.0.0.66`, 24 failures |
| `success_after_failures` | 1 finding, user `administrator` |
| `password_spray` | 0 findings |

Rows that must be **ignored entirely**:

| Row | Why |
|---|---|
| `LogonType 2` | Physical console logon. Nobody is at the keyboard attacking over the network. |
| `LogonType 5` | A service starting. Constant on any Windows machine. |
| `EventID 4672` | "Special privileges assigned" — not an authentication attempt. Present to confirm the parser skips unknown IDs rather than choking. |

`SubStatus 0xC0000064` means the account does not exist, which is the Windows
equivalent of sshd's "invalid user" and is how username enumeration is distinguished
from password guessing on this platform.
